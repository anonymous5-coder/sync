## 1. Purpose and governing principles

This single plan consolidates the investigations, scientific verification requirements, mathematical audits, experiments, and edition tasks. It replaces the need for multiple supporting Markdown files.

The work preserves these names exactly:
- **Space or Null Theory**
- **Out of Box Theory**
- **Recursive Intelligence**
- **ROOT Control Protocol**

The purpose is to improve precision, evidence, internal consistency, falsifiability, and practical usefulness—not to make the theories appear more established than the evidence permits.

### Epistemic classification

Every major claim in the two documents must be classified as one of:

1. **Established result** — supported by mature theory or replicated evidence within a stated scope.
2. **Definition** — a meaning adopted by this framework.
3. **Derived proposition** — follows from stated premises and valid reasoning.
4. **Empirical hypothesis** — a testable claim that could fail.
5. **Modelling assumption** — a simplifying assumption used to construct a model.
6. **Speculative extension** — plausible but not sufficiently established.
7. **Personal measurement convention** — a practical tracker definition, not a validated instrument.

Assign one primary epistemic classification to each claim; add secondary tags where a claim genuinely combines distinct kinds of support. Do not force a mathematical result, empirical interpretation and personal measurement convention into one undifferentiated status.

Mathematical notation does not validate an empirical claim. A theorem establishes only what follows from its premises. A deduction from a model does not establish that the model accurately represents reality. Negative and contradictory results must be retained.

## 3. The eight investigations — all retained

### I. State-Space Completeness
**Question:** Do the current variables adequately represent the causes and predictors of the target behavioural transitions?

Investigate:
- [ ] define the target behaviour and context;
- [ ] define cue, urge, fatigue/physiological load, task difficulty/ambiguity, goal activation, habit strength and competing-action accessibility;
- [ ] consider emotional state, working-memory load, attention, reward expectation, sleep/circadian state and external demands;
- [ ] distinguish latent constructs from observable indicators;
- [ ] identify overlap, redundancy, confounding and omitted variables;
- [ ] compare parsimonious and expanded models;
- [ ] test incremental predictive value out of sample.

**Deliverable within the existing documents:** canonical variable dictionary, operational definitions, and mappings between full and reduced state vectors.

### II. Causal Identification
**Question:** Which factors actually change action probability, distraction duration and recovery?

Investigate:
- [ ] causal diagrams for cue, state, task, action, consequence and outcome;
- [ ] confounders, mediators, moderators and reverse causality;
- [ ] pre-specified predictions and primary outcomes;
- [ ] randomized or counterbalanced within-person changes where safe and feasible;
- [ ] obstruction, task-friction reduction, override and combined interventions;
- [ ] intervention fidelity and alternative explanations;
- [ ] null, adverse and contradictory results.

Do not infer causation from an uncontrolled before/after comparison alone.

### III. Dynamic Behavioural Modelling
**Question:** How do state, behaviour and policy evolve over seconds, days and months?

Investigate:
- [ ] fast, intermediate and slow processes;
- [ ] discrete, continuous and hybrid models;
- [ ] thresholds, hysteresis, adaptation, reinforcement and recovery;
- [ ] exogenous disturbances and partially observed state;
- [ ] whether history variables are needed or a Markov assumption is defensible;
- [ ] static versus dynamic prediction;
- [ ] parameter identifiability, sensitivity and out-of-sample performance.

### IV. Measurement Validity
**Question:** Do the tracker ratings and logs measure the constructs they claim to measure?

Investigate:
- [ ] behavioural anchors for each 0–5 rating;
- [ ] consistency, repeatability and rating drift;
- [ ] subjective reports versus observable proxies;
- [ ] timestamp and sampling rules;
- [ ] missingness, uncertainty, floor/ceiling effects and measurement reactivity;
- [ ] separation of raw measures from composites.

Unvalidated ratings remain exploratory, not clinical or diagnostic measures.

### V. Longitudinal Stability and Retention
**Question:** Do improvements persist, generalize and survive reduced environmental support?

Investigate:
- [ ] acquisition, maintenance, transfer and relapse/recovery as separate outcomes;
- [ ] performance while interventions are active;
- [ ] reduced-support or withdrawal phases where safe;
- [ ] rebound, substitution and compensatory behaviour;
- [ ] sleep, fatigue, workload, burden and functional output;
- [ ] sustainable maintenance criteria.

Do not claim permanent change without adequate follow-up.

### VI. Individual Differences and Personalization
**Question:** Why do interventions work differently across people or across states of one person?

Investigate:
- [ ] sleep, stress, task type, learning history, environment, goals and accessibility as potential moderators;
- [ ] stable person-level differences versus temporary state differences;
- [ ] personalized rules versus fixed interventions;
- [ ] over-personalization and intervention burden;
- [ ] boundaries requiring professional assessment rather than self-diagnosis.

Begin with within-person evidence. Population claims require replication across participants.

### VII. Predictive Validation
**Question:** Can the framework forecast a target action or failure before it occurs, better than a simple baseline?

Investigate:
- [ ] target event and prediction horizon;
- [ ] naive baselines such as historical rate or recent average;
- [ ] frozen predictors and time-ordered holdout periods;
- [ ] calibration, discrimination and uncertainty;
- [ ] comparison with simpler models and ablations;
- [ ] drift when routines or environments change.

Retrospective explanation is not prospective prediction.

### VIII. Generalization and Falsifiability
**Question:** What evidence would show that a component is incomplete, false or less useful than an alternative?

Investigate:
- [ ] at least one possible falsifier for every major empirical claim;
- [ ] competing models and discriminating tests;
- [ ] boundary conditions;
- [ ] separation of logical propositions from empirical claims about brains and humans;
- [ ] independent replication and adversarial review;
- [ ] retention of negative results and unresolved disagreements.

## 4. Theory-specific research questions

### 4.1 Space or Null Theory
- [ ] Is context adequately decomposed into external, internal, structural and goal-related components?
- [ ] What exactly is absent in a “null” condition?
- [ ] Which operations remain possible without new external input?
- [ ] How do architecture, memory and initial conditions constrain internal processing?
- [ ] Does the theory make any testable prediction beyond established contextual and computational principles?
- [ ] Keep operational null distinct from vacuum and absolute nothingness.

### 4.2 Out of Box Theory
- [ ] Distinguish novelty relative to a system, explicit representation, logical derivability, probabilistic support and empirical discovery.
- [ ] Check soundness assumptions and possible-world entailment.
- [ ] Distinguish deduction from induction, abduction and prediction.
- [ ] Examine information-theoretic and algorithmic descriptions of implicit structure and generated output.
- [ ] Test prospective predictions using held-out or independently measured target data.
- [ ] Identify the boundary where multiple worlds remain compatible with available information.

### 4.3 Physical–cognitive bridge
- [ ] Verify neural energetics and brain metabolism using authoritative neuroscience and biophysics literature.
- [ ] State Landauer's assumptions; do not convert its bit-erasure bound into an energy-per-thought estimate.
- [ ] Distinguish thermodynamic entropy, Shannon entropy, variational free energy, cognitive effort and distraction.
- [ ] Treat cost-sensitive action selection as a computational model, not a direct theorem of thermodynamics.
- [ ] Formalize multi-stable dynamical systems and state-space attractor landscapes for embodied behavioural and habitual loops; define state variables, dynamics, attractors and basin boundaries.
- [ ] Define attractor-basin depth and transition-barrier heights only where the selected model supports a defensible landscape or potential; do not assume every nonlinear system has a scalar potential.
- [ ] Analyse state-transition stability and transition likelihood under noise, fatigue and other perturbations, including timescales, observables, uncertainty and falsifiers.
- [ ] Distinguish dynamical-systems stability and model-specific landscape quantities from thermodynamic entropy, Shannon entropy and thermodynamic free energy unless a valid mapping is derived and supported.
- [ ] Test behavioural consequences independently from physical substrate constraints.

### 4.4 Recursive Intelligence
- [ ] Define levels: self-monitoring, self-prediction, error attribution, policy update, goal revision and controller redesign.
- [ ] Distinguish metacognition from consciousness and subjective experience.
- [ ] Investigate accuracy and calibration of the self-model.
- [ ] Address metric gaming, Goodhart effects, goal misspecification and harmful self-modification.
- [ ] Compare fixed policies, feedback policies and adaptive meta-controllers.

### 4.5 ROOT Control Protocol
- [ ] Specify the intended use case and limits.
- [ ] Define each stage's input, operation, output and feedback.
- [ ] Test which stages are necessary, redundant or context-dependent.
- [ ] Determine whether the seven-stage cycle is sequential, iterative or concurrent.
- [ ] Add fidelity, burden, adverse-effect and stopping criteria.
- [ ] Define when the protocol is insufficient and outside support is appropriate.

### 12.5 Conclusion and knowledge-status audit

Use claim-specific standards. No single validation method applies to every kind of claim.

For each major conclusion, record the applicable checks:
1. **Semantic adequacy:** are the terms and scope sufficiently clear?
2. **Logical validity:** does the conclusion follow from the premises?
3. **Mathematical validity:** are formal derivations and model results correct under stated assumptions?
4. **Empirical correspondence:** do observations support the claim within the measured scope?
5. **Predictive performance:** does it make successful prospective predictions against suitable baselines, where prediction is claimed?
6. **Causal adequacy:** is causal language justified by the design and evidence, where causation is claimed?
7. **Reproducibility and independence:** can the result be independently checked or reproduced where applicable?
8. **Competing-explanation assessment:** have plausible alternative explanations and counterexamples been considered?
9. **Boundary and uncertainty:** are limits, exceptions, sensitivity and underdetermination stated?
10. **Revisability:** is there a clear condition under which the claim would be revised, narrowed or rejected?

- [ ] Apply only the relevant validation checks to each claim; do not demand empirical tests of pure definitions or treat formal validity as empirical confirmation.
- [ ] Distinguish mathematical truth within a formal system from the truth of the system's assumptions about the world.
- [ ] Distinguish empirical support from proof, absence of disproof from confirmation, and explanatory coherence from empirical adequacy.
- [ ] State the confidence and evidence quality at the level justified; avoid a single global label that hides mixed claim statuses.
- [ ] Record unresolved underdetermination and competing interpretations rather than forcing a definitive conclusion.
- [ ] Identify which conclusions are established, formally demonstrated, empirically supported, provisionally supported, unresolved, contradicted or rejected.
- [ ] Ensure the final treatise does not present a proposed unifying framework as a demonstrated universal law.

**Required deliverable:** a conclusion-status register and a concise final epistemic statement that explains what the treatise does and does not establish.

## 13. Verification standards and evidence-to-claim traceability

This section governs how the research team documents support. It supplements, and does not replace, the epistemic classifications in Section 1 or the domain-specific investigations in Sections 3–6.

### 13.1 Evidence-to-claim traceability

- [ ] Assign stable identifiers to all substantive entries in the claim inventory; distinguish formal-result identifiers from empirical/source-supported claim identifiers.
- [ ] Link each claim to the exact supporting source location, dataset, derivation or experiment.
- [ ] Record source type, publication status, date, methods, sample or domain, limitations and conflicts of interest where material.
- [ ] Distinguish primary evidence from reviews, textbooks, commentary and secondary summaries.
- [ ] Verify that each cited source actually supports the precise wording attached to it.
- [ ] Record contrary evidence and sources that support a narrower or different conclusion.
- [ ] Keep source absence, inaccessible evidence and failed verification visible; do not fabricate citations or silently substitute weaker support.

### 13.2 Formal proof verification

- [ ] Check definitions, axiom consistency where relevant, premise scope, inference rules and every proof step.
- [ ] Search for hidden assumptions, circular reasoning, quantifier changes, domain shifts and invalid generalization.
- [ ] Preserve “proves” and “establishes” when the formal derivation is valid and the conclusion necessarily follows in the specified system.
- [ ] State whether a result is conditional on axioms, definitions, model assumptions or an interpretation.
- [ ] Keep formal proof status separate from the empirical adequacy of the model or assumptions.

### 13.3 Empirical and causal claim verification

- [ ] Define the observable outcome, measurement procedure, target population or individual, setting and time horizon.
- [ ] Evaluate design quality, sampling, confounding, measurement error, missingness, statistical uncertainty and external validity as applicable.
- [ ] Distinguish descriptive, predictive, mechanistic and causal claims.
- [ ] Match causal language to identification assumptions and design strength.
- [ ] Report null, mixed and adverse results with the same visibility as positive results.
- [ ] Do not generalize an individual tracker result to populations without independent evidence.

### 13.4 Review and reproducibility

- [ ] Ensure equations, calculations, cited passages and experimental analyses can be independently checked.
- [ ] Keep enough method and data provenance to reproduce material results where legally and practically possible.
- [ ] Record reviewer objections and their disposition; do not treat agreement or authority as a substitute for evidence.
- [ ] Perform a final adversarial review focused on the strongest claims, weakest bridges and most consequential assumptions.
- [ ] Obtain an independent review from a suitably qualified reviewer where feasible; if unavailable, label the adversarial review as internal and do not describe it as independent.
- [ ] Separate unresolved reviewer disagreement from verified error.
